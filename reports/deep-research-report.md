# Predicting MCI and dementia from blood CpG methylation

## Executive summary

The recent literature on predicting mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, or dementia from **blood or peripheral DNA methylation** is promising but still relatively immature. There are fewer true **prediction** papers than association/EWAS papers, most cohorts are still modest in size, external validation is uncommon, and the very best headline AUCs often come from **multimodal** models that combine methylation with stronger established biomarkers such as **plasma pTau181**, **CSF**, or **MRI**, rather than from methylation alone. The largest blood EWAS / cross-tissue work in this area prioritised only a handful of reproducible blood CpGs across ADNI and AIBL, underscoring that stable signal in peripheral methylation is real but sparse and hard-won. citeturn31search6turn44search1

Among the more convincing diagnostic studies, **small-panel regularised or logistic models** have held up better than more complicated models when judged by interpretability and validation. A targeted Japanese methylation-capture study validated five DMRs and achieved **AUC 0.81** in a held-out validation set using **ANKH + MARS methylation + APOE ε4**, versus **0.77** for APOE alone. A blood-based late-onset AD study combining four methylation markers with **plasma pTau181** and **APOE ε4** reported **AUC 0.93**, **83.3% sensitivity**, and **90.0% specificity**, outperforming a pTau181-only model at **AUC 0.85**. By contrast, a large lncRNA-methylation signature achieved **AUC 0.79** in training but dropped to **0.67** and **0.60** on external AddNeuroMed and ADNI validation cohorts, which is exactly the kind of train-to-test degradation expected in small-n/high-p methylation problems. citeturn39view0turn45view3turn45view4turn45view5turn41view0

Deep learning can look spectacular internally, but the strongest numbers are best treated cautiously. A multi-task deep autoencoder on longitudinal ADNI blood methylation reported median **AUC 0.996** for **CN→MCI** and **0.787** for **MCI→AD**, but the positive CN→MCI class was only **44 converters**, and the evaluation was based on repeated internal train/test splits rather than an independent external cohort. Likewise, a graph convolutional network integrating blood methylation with other modalities achieved **AUC 0.917** for **AD vs non-AD**, but this was a **multimodal** ten-fold CV result with **CSF** contributing heavily to performance; it is not evidence that blood methylation alone is sufficient to reach that level. citeturn27view0turn28view0turn29view0turn22view0turn24view1turn24view3

For a dataset like this — **n ≈ 190**, **p ≈ 2,000–6,000**, clear class imbalance, and only **two time points** — the literature and current best practice repeatedly surface **regularised linear models**, **latent-space models**, **kernel methods after dimension reduction**, and **small deep networks** as the main families explored in blood methylation prediction. The elastic-net family is particularly well suited to **p > n** settings and correlated predictors, which is exactly the geometry of methylation data. citeturn47search1turn47search5turn47search17turn20view3

A few broader conclusions fall out of this table. First, **truly external** validation is rare, and when it happens the AUC usually falls materially compared with the training cohort, as in the lncRNA paper. Second, the strongest numbers often reflect **extra modalities**, not methylation acting alone. Third, most successful panels end up surprisingly **small**: four markers, five DMRs, fifteen lncRNA-derived features, or seventeen CpGs, not thousands. Fourth, some of the best-designed papers are still underpowered by mainstream ML standards, which means methodological discipline matters at least as much as model selection. citeturn41view0turn39view0turn24view1turn45view5

## Methodological themes

Across the reviewed studies, a recurring workflow appears: sample and probe QC, normalisation, probe filtering, batch handling, cell-composition adjustment, and then a heavily reduced feature set. The AddNeuroMed blood EWAS is representative, combining probe filtering, dasen normalisation, Houseman cell estimates, and age/sex/bisulphite adjustments before testing diagnosis effects. The `minfi` and `sva` ecosystems recur throughout array-based methylation work, while sequencing-based studies use parallel tools such as Trim Galore, Bismark, and MethylKit. citeturn33view1turn33view2turn46search0turn46search1turn38view1turn39view0

Dimensionality reduction is usually aggressive. Published models often retain only a handful of CpGs, DMRs, proxy scores, or latent components: five DMRs in the Japanese MC-seq study, seventeen CpGs in the Qian et al. classifier, fifteen lncRNA-derived features in the Clinical Epigenetics study, and 1,000 CpGs in the deep-autoencoder study after starting from a much larger background set. Feature reduction appears in several forms, including variance filtering, limma-style supervised ranking, elastic net, PLS/sPLS-DA, Boruta, and RF-RFE. citeturn39view0turn24view3turn41view0turn30view0turn20view1turn20view3

Validation is typically internal. Repeated train/test splits, nested cross-validation, or 10-fold CV dominate the literature, while independent external cohorts remain comparatively rare. The strongest internal numbers are therefore often best read as feasibility estimates rather than transportable clinical performance. ROC-AUC is the most common headline metric, with PR-AUC, calibration, and threshold-sensitive metrics less frequently reported. citeturn27view0turn28view0turn24view3turn45view3turn45view4

Model families reported in the literature cluster around four broad groups. Regularised linear models and logistic regression are common in the strongest targeted panels and proxy-score models. Latent-space methods such as PLS and sparse PLS-DA appear frequently when the feature space is highly collinear. Tree ensembles often serve as feature selectors or comparison models rather than final end-models. Deep learning appears mainly in the larger ADNI-style studies and often relies on small internal samples or multimodal inputs, which complicates direct comparison with blood-only prediction. citeturn39view0turn20view3turn41view0turn29view0

The main limitations are familiar across the field: leakage from full-dataset preprocessing, confounding by blood-cell composition and technical batch, cohort drift across assay platforms, and a persistent gap between internal CV results and independent validation. These issues are especially pronounced for prodromal outcomes such as CN→MCI, where class counts are small and performance estimates can be unstable. citeturn41view0turn24view3turn33view1turn33view2
